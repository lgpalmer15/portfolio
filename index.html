BESS · SCADA integration

An in-house battery scheduling platform

$200K per yearthird-party costs eliminated
+5%project revenue
The problem
Nexamp relied on outside vendors to schedule its battery energy storage (BESS) assets. That was expensive, and it left value on the table in how the batteries charged and discharged.
What I did
I led an internal scheduling platform that integrated directly with the SCADA systems that control the batteries. I worked with operations, engineering and data science to write the PRD, define an MVP framework, and plan a phased rollout that met operators' needs without risking live assets.
What changed
Nexamp cut $200K per year in third-party costs and raised project revenue 5% through better asset operations.
Customer experience · Billing

One bill for community solar customers

2 →to 1bills per customer, often a month apart before
4success metrics set before launch: usage, call volume, cancellations, NPS
The problem
Community solar lets people subscribe to a nearby solar farm instead of putting panels on their roof. The utility applies solar credits to the customer's electric bill, and Nexamp bills separately for those credits at a discount. Customers got two bills, often a month apart, and had to match them up themselves to see what they were saving. Confusing bills lead to support calls, and frustrated customers cancel.
What I did
I led the systems infrastructure for a consolidated bill: one statement that combines a customer's utility usage with their Nexamp solar credits. That meant bringing utility data into both our customer-facing and internal products, and it had to be exactly right. I worked across utilities, Customer Success, RevOps, Marketing and Engineering to validate every data flow before a bill went out. I also owned the single-bill sign-up in customer portal onboarding. Before launch, I defined how we would measure success, then set up a staged beta tracked in Salesforce so we could check each step before expanding.
What changed
Customers get one clear statement instead of two. Happier customers mean higher NPS and fewer support calls and cancellations.
Systems integration · Operations

A single source of truth for $30M of inventory

$120K+saved in the pilot
$30Minventory brought into one system
The problem
As the first PM for operations and integrations, I found teams tracking $30M of solar construction inventory across disconnected systems, spreadsheets and email.
What I did
I interviewed every group that touched inventory: Construction Management, Finance and Supply. Each group described a different symptom, but the real problem was that there was no shared source of truth. The pilot saved more than $120K, which justified the full rollout.

Every team described a different symptom. The real problem was the one they all shared.

Education

Curriculum Associates · Associate PM →to PM · 2015–2021
Assessment · Zero to one

i-Ready Quick Checks

9 teamscoordinated from concept to launch, plus partner Learnosity
On timelaunch date held by shipping grades 2–5 first, then K–1
The problem
Curriculum Associates was moving from supplemental programs into core curriculum with i-Ready Classroom, and it needed a new kind of assessment. Existing i-Ready assessments were long, and teachers told us students took too long to finish them. Quick Checks had to be short, tied directly to the curriculum, and easy for teachers to assign.
What I did
I owned Quick Checks from concept to launch, including the teacher assignment workflow and the student test-taking experience. I coordinated nine teams, from psychometrics and content authors to development, reporting and go-to-market. Early UX research showed teachers would use Quick Checks as quick formative checks, not mastery tests, so I made the case for letting teachers build their own quizzes question by question. The psychometrics team pushed back at first, since fewer than six questions can't measure mastery, but the research won them over. Kindergarten and first-grade students needed new ways to interact on screen, so I wrote specs for our content platform partner, Learnosity, which built almost every feature we requested. To protect the launch date, I released grades 2–5 first and K–1 shortly after.
What changed
Teachers adopted Quick Checks quickly, especially in grades 2–5, and told us they valued being trusted to decide what worked for their classroom. Some assigned a single question every day, others a weekly topic check or a unit quiz, and the product handled all of them.
Discovery · Product ethics

A reading and spoken-word assessment without auto-scored audio

1 featurecut after discovery
Fasterdelivery and stronger adoption
The problem
We were building a first-of-its-kind literacy assessment in which students read aloud. The plan assumed software would score the audio automatically.
What I did
I led discovery through customer interviews and competitive analysis. Reading specialists, the experts the product had to win over, told us clearly that they didn't trust automated scoring of a child's reading. I used that research to cut the feature and design educator-scored review instead.
What changed
The development timeline got shorter and adoption improved, because the product respected the professional judgment of the educators it served.

Sometimes the best product decision is the feature you decide not to build.

Built with Claude & Lovable

An AI meal-planning app that plans the week as a whole

Soloproblem definition through launch
Testedwith friends and family, then iterated
The problem
Most meal planners treat each recipe on its own. In real kitchens, prep done early in the week should feed later meals, and the grocery list should build itself.
What I did
I owned problem definition, MVP scope and design. Claude helped me frame the specs, and Lovable generated the app. Early plans were generic and repetitive, skipped prep steps, and sometimes paired odd ingredients and cuisines. I rewrote the prompts and added guardrails so the model combined steps across recipes and produced more sensible pairings. I tested it with friends and family, then added adjustable meal counts and portions and cut input steps for the MVP.
What changed
The app links early-week prep to later meals and builds the grocery list automatically. I included feedback from friends and family to improve the experience. Support for dietary restrictions is next.

Human judgment about what to build and why it matters remains irreplaceable.

How I work

Four habits behind the results.

Discovery

Listen past the request.

At Nexamp, I interviewed Construction Management, Finance and Supply, every group that touched inventory. Each one described a different pain point, but they all came from the same gap. No one shared a single source of truth. I start by finding the real problem.

Analytics

Watch what people actually do.

FullStory session data showed community solar customers abandoning sign-up at extra steps and signature requests. I coached a PM on my team through a redesign that cut steps and consolidated signatures, and completion rates went up. Behavior shows where the friction really is.

Evidence

Set success criteria before building.

I define pilots with success and stop criteria, run staged betas, and measure after launch. The $120K inventory pilot is why the full rollout was approved. I decide what would change my mind before I start building.

Prioritization

Make tradeoffs visible.

At Nexamp I introduced a transparent framework that scored competing VP requests by company impact. Leaders trusted it even when their own features were deprioritized, because they could see the reasoning.

Working with me

In the room when it counts.

Results matter, and so does how the team feels getting there.

The day a release broke

Owning the miss, together.

A big release went live with major issues. I couldn't fix the code, but I could stay in the room with the engineers. I tracked down problems on the front end, kept leadership updated so the team could focus, and kept morale up until it was fixed.

I owned the failure with leadership and didn't point fingers.

The team gets the wins. I own the misses.

Relationships
I get to know the people I work with beyond their job titles, and I learn how their work connects to mine. That trust holds a team together when the work gets hard and messy. I try to keep it fun, especially then.
Transparency
I share news early and often, good or bad. Roadmap updates go out regularly, problems get flagged before they become surprises, and meetings end with clear outcomes, owners and action items.
Curiosity
I learn how the work is actually done, whether it's a teacher's, a field operator's, or an engineer's. Walking a mile in someone's shoes is how I find the real problem, and how I earn their trust.
Fast learning
I learn complex domains quickly, from state education requirements to utility data to Salesforce and SCADA integrations.
Collaboration
I'm quick to lend a hand, review a draft, or brainstorm. I do my best thinking with peers who share ideas freely, and I help build that culture on every team I join.
